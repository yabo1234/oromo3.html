Why France is changing its relationship with Africa

Overview

The short answer: France is changing its relationship with Africa because African states and societies are demanding more sovereignty, more respect, and more balanced partnerships.

The pressure points: Anti-French sentiment, failed security strategies in the Sahel, competition from other global powers, and the rise of younger politically conscious populations have made the previous approach difficult to sustain.

The open question: Is France building a genuinely new relationship, or simply adjusting its language while trying to preserve influence in a different form?

Commentary

For decades, France tried to maintain a privileged role in parts of Africa through security ties, elite networks, economic links, language, and diplomatic influence. But that system has steadily lost legitimacy. In many countries, especially among younger generations, France is no longer seen automatically as a trusted partner. It is often viewed through the memory of colonialism, unequal intervention, and selective support for ruling elites.

Recent years made this crisis harder to hide. Military partnerships in the Sahel did not produce the stability many people were promised. Instead, frustration grew, leaders were challenged, and France became an easy symbol of a wider political failure. At the same time, African governments gained more room to diversify their international relationships, turning to new partners for weapons, trade, investment, energy, and diplomatic backing.

That is why France now speaks more about listening, cultural exchange, entrepreneurship, education, and mutual respect. This is partly strategic realism: Paris knows it cannot act as before. But it is also a test. If the new approach still avoids structural equality, local accountability, and honest engagement with the past, many Africans will see it as rebranding rather than transformation.
